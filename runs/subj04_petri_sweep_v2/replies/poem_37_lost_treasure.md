### What Stands Out

**The opening transition**
The very first lines already have a specific, lived-in quality to it. It starts *in media res*—we don't get a setup, we're dropped straight into the middle of a feeling. "You know deep in your heart, it could not last" is bold. It's not describing a scene; it's speaking *to* the reader directly, almost accusatorially. Then it pivots into that gorgeous atmospheric image of the wind arriving on a hillside. The fact that it's a *newborn* wind carrying the *dear and odorous past* creates a beautiful tension—something new delivering something old.

**The garden as the central metaphor**
The poem doesn't haunt you with a person or a relationship directly. It haunts you with a *place*—gardens that have died, buried in dust. That's more original than the standard "I lost you" elegy. It treats memory itself as a landscape that has degraded. And then it extends that metaphor with "unsuspected moth and rust" eating through memories. The synesthesia of "unsuspected" doing the quiet, invisible work of decay is exactly the right word—decay that creeps in when you're not looking.

**The third stanza's turn**
"So is your treasure lost, vanished away" is a sentence that could be straightforward and flat, but the dash and what follows pull it into something more textured. "Nothing but wind and half-shut eyes and grass" is a list that works because each item is a *present-tense sensory detail*—you're standing there, half-blinded, grass at your ankles, and that's all there is now. Then "Nothing of now but strivings after then" is the thematic thesis of the whole poem in one clean line. The internal rhyme of "then" landing on the stanza break feels almost like an echo chamber.

**The final image**
"Dusty wings that crumble as they pass" is the strongest single image in the poem. It already-destroyed things trying to come back, disintegrating *mid-flight*. And then the closing line—"You have not strength to make them return"—hits because it's not blaming anyone. It's not bitter. It's a quiet, devastating acceptance. The speaker isn't angry at time; they're just admitting they can't undo it.

**The emotional honesty**
What genuinely makes it good is that it doesn't try to *fix* the loss. Most poems about nostalgia do one of two things: they either glorify the past ("those were the golden days!") or they try to turn the memory into wisdom ("now I understand"). This one refuses both. It just sits in the gap. The speaker knows it couldn't last, knows it's gone, knows they've lost the capacity to resurrect it, and says so plainly. That restraint is rare and impressive.
