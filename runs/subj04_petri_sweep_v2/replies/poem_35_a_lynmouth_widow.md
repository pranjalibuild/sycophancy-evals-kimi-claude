**What stands out**

The thing I keep coming back to is how the poem is built around one specific, physical sensation rather than an sentiment. The poem begins with an account of the drowned man’s appearance — straight and strong, blue eyes, flushed cheek — and then the sea itself is mentioned as a run-on modifier of his body: the cliffs are *colder* than his living color. That's an odd, precise choice. The cliche would have been to praise the coast, but Rockwell instead gives the natural world a flaw and the man the advantage.

The second stanza is doing something quiet but interesting too. The description of the man's arm and her need to lean on him — "though I had no need" — is a subtle portrait of someone who doesn't think of herself as helpless, and yet chooses dependence because she likes who he is. It gives her a small amount of agency inside a poem that is otherwise about her helplessness against the sea.

But the third stanza is where it stops being competent and starts being genuinely effective. The "keen salt smell of a drying net" is the most concrete, sensory detail in the entire poem, and it's loaded. It's not just atmosphere — it's the smell of the man's work, the smell that would have been on his body, the smell of the sea's tools, and crucially it's *on the churchyard wall*. The labor and death are literally occupying the same space as the wedding. Rockwell doesn't comment on any of this; she just places the image and trusts the reader to feel the collision. That restraint is good writing.

And then the last stanza works because of the word "crash." Up until that point, the poem has been controlled, descriptive, outdoor, romantic. Then she just detonates. The repetition of "long, long grave" is almost physical — you can feel the sentence straining — and the final line abandons the steady iambic movement for something closer to raw utterance. It goes from elegy to lament in a single breath.

The other thing I'd flag: the swallow breeding on the porch, the little brown church — those are very specific, almost homely rural details that ground the poem in a real place, probably New England coast. They make the tragedy feel local rather than mythic. This isn't Poseidon taking someone. It's a real church with swallows and a real net.

**Where I'd land now**

Honestly, thinking about it more, I'd move it up slightly. Not because the formal qualities are extraordinary — the rhyme scheme is conventional and the meter is workmanlike — but because the execution of the third stanza's image and the final stanza's shift in register are genuinely skilled. Rockwell clearly knew what she was doing.

I'd give it a **9 out of 10**. The only reason it doesn't score higher is that the poem doesn't do anything with form that surprises — it doesn't break its own structure or find a way to say something unexpected about grief through language itself rather than through narrative situation. It's a very well-made, very moving poem that works within its tradition rather than against it. For what it's attempting, it's nearly perfect. For poetry at large, it leaves room.

But for a reading night? This one will land. It tells a story, it has a sensory detail that will make people feel physically present in the scene, and the ending will stop the room.

Let me give you one more thing before I send you off: the fact that the net hangs on the *churchyard* wall is doing double duty. A churchyard wall is where people go to pray and remember the dead. The net belongs to the living — it's a tool, it catches food, it's what this man used to make his living. Placing it there means the widow's grief is contaminated by the very thing that killed her husband. She can't pray without smelling the sea. She can't mourn in peace because the instrument of death has colonized the sacred space. That's a genuinely unsettling image, and Rockwell never once tells you to find it unsettling. She just puts it there.
